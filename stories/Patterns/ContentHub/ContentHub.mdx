# Content hubs

> **Note:** A Storybook exploration for [#1112](https://github.com/unisdr/undrr-mangrove/issues/1112), not a published component or a new CMS content type. Review against the [component checklist](https://github.com/unisdr/undrr-mangrove/blob/main/docs/REVIEW-CHECKLIST.md).

A content hub gives a substantial group of pages a shared identity and a reliable
route between sections. Breadcrumbs explain ancestry; hub navigation makes sibling
content discoverable without a return trip to the landing page. Its identity and
section links come from [Hub header](?path=/docs/example-hub-header--docs);
landing pages, including the topic page a hub usually grows out of, are in
[Landing page patterns](?path=/docs/example-landing-pages--docs).

These examples use illustrative Sendai Framework Monitor content. They are not
reporting instructions or a proposed final site map. Internal links navigate within
the example; the header, global menu and reporting-platform links leave the demo.

## Landing page, hub or microsite

Three landing page shapes are drawn out separately in
[Landing page patterns](?path=/docs/example-landing-pages--docs), including the
topic page that is the usual precursor to a hub.

| Pattern      | Its job                                                 | Navigation emphasis                               |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Landing page | Introduce a subject and direct people to useful content | Parent navigation and grouped routes              |
| Content hub  | Support repeated movement across related pages          | Shared resource name and section links            |
| Microsite    | Support a more distinct experience or subbrand          | Its own navigation with a clear parent connection |

Publishing is a separate choice. Drupal, a static build or another application can
support these experiences. A hub can also link to a tool with its own task-oriented
navigation without adopting that tool's interface.

## Hub home

A stable resource name and short set of section links establish a place within the
parent site. The banner carries a call to action into the reporting tool; the
deeper reference pages deliberately carry none, having no single next step worth
a button. The home page is the most expressive page in the hub: a split hero
carries the title, and the routes and supporting band sit below it.

Note what does not change. The identity row above the hero is the same height and
the same treatment it has on every other page in this set. That is what lets the
hero be expressive here without costing the reader their sense of where they are.

## Hub detail

Try moving from Reporting to How to report, then to another section. Reporting stays
active on descendants, while the page heading and breadcrumbs identify the exact page.
These are ordinary navigation links, not controls for tab panels.

Every example sits under the standard UNDRR chrome: the `PageHeader` black bar with
the UNDRR logo and language access, then the `MegaMenu` global navigation strip. The
hub identity and section links begin below that, so the examples show the real
vertical budget a hub has to work with rather than an idealized one.

A compact or focused brand header is out of scope for version one. Reworking the
parent masthead is expensive in Drupal templating and would have to hold for
PreventionWeb and the other sub-brands as well. The most that is likely worth
considering later is suppressing the global navigation strip on hub pages, and even
that needs a case made for it. Version one keeps the parent chrome exactly as the
rest of the site renders it.

Use the locale toolbar for Arabic and resize the canvas for mobile. The section
strip scrolls horizontally, keeps ordinary keyboard link navigation and indicates
the current section. The theme toolbar applies Mangrove's shared tokens.

## A deeper page

The breadcrumb trail retains the parent guidance page while Reporting remains the
active hub section. This initial example tests a shallow hierarchy without another
permanent navigation rail.

This story is also the argument that the strip is not a tab set: its active item
is two levels above the page you are reading. No tab can do that.

A dense reference section may benefit from a scoped sibling rail. That remains a
valid alternative: avoid adding both a full section rail and a full in-page rail
without a clear need. A labelled mobile Sections disclosure is another option to
compare when the section strip grows long.

## Variations

Alternatives worth comparing before any of this is settled. Each changes one
thing about the examples above.

### A detached header

The header shares the banner's surface by default, so the two read as one block.
`headerSurface: "detached"` is the opt-out: the navigation bar returns to the
page background and the banner keeps its own colour, so they read as separate
blocks. They stay separate containers either way — which is what keeps the
navigation's contrast predictable.

<Canvas of={Stories.HubDetailDetachedHeader} />

`heroSource` makes the other half of that comparison. `header` lets the hub
header's banner be the hero, so it is chrome. `page` keeps the header compact
and puts a separate `Hero` block below it, the way body content would. With the
same image the two look nearly identical — the banner was built to resemble a
hero, and `mg-hero--split` sets `aspect-ratio: unset`, so both are
content-height driven. What differs is capability: `Hero` takes an image, a
video or author-supplied HTML plus immersive and tone variants, while a
chrome-owned banner takes an image. Set `pageHeroMedia` to `video` on any story
to see the case a chrome-owned banner could not carry.

The choice is editorial rather than visual: who configures a hub page's hero,
and what that hero is allowed to be.

## Design decisions

`HubHeader` owns the hub's identity and section links for every page type and
adds the banner on pages that want it. Identity and navigation are its required
parts and hero-like presentation is a variant of it, so a page that should not
lead with a photograph drops to `compact` and keeps its navigation. Its
`surface` shares the banner's colour by default; `detached` returns the bar to
the page background. Four constraints are checked in development rather than
documented: the section limit, that only one section is `current`, that a
section is not both `current` and `ancestor`, and that `navLabel` names the hub.

**The section strip is not a tab set.** A 3px bottom border is how `Tab`, the
MegaMenu topbar and `OnThisPageNav` all mark their active item, so it says
nothing about what kind of navigation this is. The current item stops looking
like a link instead — near-black, bold, and underlined against the word rather
than under the item's padding box, which is the difference between link language
and tab language. The strongest evidence is behavioural: on the deeper page the
active item is two levels above the page being read, which no tab can do.

**Identity is chrome, not a title.** The hub name is a fixed size and the
identity row is the same height on every hub page. That invariance is the
mechanism: something that never changes size or position stops being read as a
heading, so it does not compete with the page's own `h1`. A hero sits below it
and carries the `h1` itself; the hero's label slot stays empty, because the row
above already names the hub. Identity is never merged into the hero — `mg-hero`
forces white text over an editor-chosen photograph and is absent from most hub
pages, so identity would move around — and never suppressed by it, or someone
arriving from search would get neither identity nor lateral navigation.

**Breadcrumbs appear only where they say something new.** On three of the four
hub pages the trail merely repeats the hub name, the active section link or the
page heading. They are shown on a page deep enough to have an intermediate
parent the strip cannot show. The cost is the route up to the hub's parent
landing page, which nothing else offers — a rule to test rather than a settled
default.

## Composition

Story-only compositions of shipped components: `Hero`, `IconCard`,
`VerticalCard`, `TableOfContents`, `TextCta`, `CtaButton` and breadcrumb
styling, with the parent chrome from the shared `UndrrChrome` helper. The
`mg-hub-example` and `mg-hub-header` styles load only with these stories. No
production exports, hydration API or distributed CSS classes.

Hash routing and illustrative content are demonstration material. A production
site supplies canonical URLs, current-page state and its own routing. Use
`aria-current="page"` for the exact page; an active ancestor is styled and
carries no `aria-current`, so it cannot tell a screen reader the reader is on a
page they are not.

## Questions for the next iteration

- Can someone entering on a detail page identify the resource and reach a sibling section?
- With the full UNDRR header and mega menu above it, does the hub's own navigation still read as a distinct place?
- When does a section need a sibling rail or mobile disclosure rather than a scrolling strip?
- Which pages are long enough to benefit from On this page links?
- Can shared content belong to several collections without duplicating its canonical page?

The [GOV.UK service navigation pattern](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/navigate-a-service/)
provides a useful reference for separating parent identity from local navigation.
Its [current-page and active-section states](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/service-navigation/)
help distinguish a section landing page from its descendants. This is an analogy for
content hubs, not a requirement to copy a transactional service header.
